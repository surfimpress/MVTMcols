# Almonte Gazette digitisation – research and development report

Prepared 24 September 2026 for Surface Impression and the Mississippi Valley Textile Museum. Covers work from the first commit on 11 April 2026 to the present.

Sources: the project's git history (436 commits), the `instructions/` knowledge base, the experiments log, the two SQLite databases, the preview outputs on disk, the Claude Code session transcripts, and the newsgrid handover briefing of 31 August. Where the record is silent or ambiguous the report says so rather than filling the gap.

## Summary

The Almonte Gazette ran weekly from 1861 to 2007. The museum holds it as roughly 70,000 scanned pages, and an earlier attempt at machine reading (an Acrobat OCR pass in 2016) produced nothing usable. The aim of this project is to turn those scans into searchable, structured text – articles, advertisements and notices as separate items, with the people, businesses, places and events they mention indexed – at a cost the museum can actually afford.

Five months of work has produced, in order: a classical image-processing pipeline that cuts every pre-1980 page into columns and advertisements, run to completion across 5,666 issues; an LLM transcription and item-classification pipeline that reads those cuts, validated on about 60 issues; a second route for the modern (post-1980) paper, where the old column model no longer applies, built on Tesseract OCR with an LLM for cleanup and segmentation; an entity and terminology layer with an external museum vocabulary; a "scaled" experiment that recovers layout structure from signals Tesseract already emits at no extra cost; and a standalone OpenCV layout analyser ("newsgrid") developed in a separate chat session and handed over at the end of August.

The single most important finding is economic. The LLM-heavy routes work well enough in quality but cost around 77,000 to 104,000 tokens and 93 seconds per page. Extrapolated across the corpus that is 5.4 to 7.3 billion tokens and roughly 76 days of continuous processing, and item segmentation alone is 72% of it. Every line of work since mid-August has been about moving that segmentation cost off the language model and onto classical geometry.

The second most important finding is about method. The project's worst regressions were shipped on the strength of improving statistics and caught only by rendering the page and looking. That lesson is now written into the project's standing instructions.

## 1. Context

### The material

The corpus index holds 7,087 issues and 70,063 pages spanning 1861 to 2007. Physically it is three different things:

- Bound volumes photographed page by page, 1860s to 1970s. Binding shadows, facing-page slivers, tears and foxing. Columns divided by printed vertical rules.
- A JBIG2 bitonal batch (1981, 1988) produced by one digitisation route.
- Greyscale 200 DPI scans (1996 to 2005) produced by another, re-wrapped as image-only PDFs.

The paper's design changed as much as its scanning. From the 1860s to the 1970s a page was five to eight columns of continuous text separated by rules, with advertisements stacked from the bottom. From some point before 1985 the paper adopted modular layout: every story is a rectangle with its own internal column count, headlines span exactly their story, photographs sit borderless inside stories, and display ads carry their own interior grids. A vertical slice down "column three" of a 1995 page cuts through several unrelated articles. That change is what split the project into two routes.

### The goal

A page image should become a set of discrete items – article, display ad, classified ad, notice, masthead, cartoon, letter, table – each with a transcript, a summary, a bounding box that can be shown as an overlay in a standards-based viewer, and a list of the named entities it mentions. The entities should reconcile across the run so that a business advertising in 1897 and 1923 is one record with two dates.

### The constraint

The museum cannot pay per-page cloud costs at this scale. All language-model work runs as subagents inside Claude Code under a subscription, never through a metered API client; the project's own notes forbid the SDK dependency. That makes tokens and wall-clock time the currencies of every design decision.

## 3. The classical cutting pipeline (pre-1980)

### What it does

For every page: profile the scan, mask the display ads, detect column boundaries, validate them, then detect headlines, body-text regions and horizontal rules. Outputs are column images, ad crops, an SVG overlay for inspection and rows in SQLite. The pipeline is deterministic and cheap – tens of milliseconds for the shared image analysis, a few seconds per page overall.

### Methods that stuck

Three nested rectangles. The PDF canvas, the scanned sheet within it and the trimmed page within that are found separately. The first step exploits a digital artefact: PDF margins are pure white in a way paper never is. That one test works on every page from 1865 to 2005.

Anchor from the clean edge, dead-reckon the binding edge. The binding side of a bound-volume scan is shadowed and unreliable; the outer edge is clean. Which side is which comes from the page number (odd is recto, binding on the left), not from measuring darkness, which misfired when a dark ad sat on the clean side. Interior column rules are found by a valley-spike-valley test on several horizontal strips of the page at 450 DPI. The outer columns, which have no rule, are projected from the interior pitch: if interior columns are 10.4% wide, so are the outer ones.

Era priors. Every successful layout is persisted, aggregated by decade, and served back as a prior for the next page of that era. Column counts change in long runs – decades at one count – so the prior is informative. A page-2 editorial template (two wide columns at 1.5 times the standard pitch, 1937 to 1952, modified after 1965) was the first entry in what was meant to be an extensible template system.

Validators biased toward keeping columns. A phantom edge column is dropped only when body-text coverage, ad overlap and headline overlap all say it is empty. An earlier version iterated the check and peeled a real 7-column page down to 3; the replacement runs once. The stated rationale: a phantom is cheap to remove by hand, a lost column is expensive.

Reinforce, don't replace. A cleaner connected-component ad detector was tried as a replacement for the contour-based one and dropped three of four ads on a test page, because the size filter wiped fragmented frame ink. It survives as a corroborating signal that boosts and adds candidates.

Two-stage ad detection with a loose second pass on low-contrast 1890s scans was "the largest single robustness win on early issues".

### Hand correction

A command-line tool (`mvtm`) lets a person or a QA subagent move boundaries, add and adjust ads, and regenerate a page, with undo and a history table. Hand-edited rows are flagged so reruns cannot clobber them. This was built after the scope discussion of 27 April settled that this app's job ends at cuts and their QA; clipping, transcription and display belong downstream.

### The campaign

Cutting the whole 1862 to 1979 run was made autonomous: a queue on disk, a LaunchAgent that keeps two cutters alive and reaps its own zombies, per-year backup to Google Drive through rclone, and an archiver that moves cold years off local disk after a 12-hour grace period. Downloads stream into the pool rather than pre-fetching. The campaign completed on 13 May.

A gated fast path extracts the embedded 1-bit bitmap from PDFs that have one instead of rasterising at a fixed DPI, writing 1-bit column and ad images at native resolution. Polarity inversion for image masks and a borrowed bounding box for unplaced masters were needed to make it safe; twenty 1962 to 1969 issues were re-cut once it was.

### Problems worth recording

The out-of-volume page edge. On 1947-01-09 the bound volume slipped out of register and a strip of an underlying page is visible past the photographed page, producing phantom columns with real but foreign text. A landscape scan embedded in a portrait PDF on the same issue's front page came out as 12 columns before per-page pitch adoption fixed it.

Merged issues. Three archive dates (1930-04-04, 1962-01-25, 1973-01-04) each hold two consecutive weekly issues scanned back to back. Two split cleanly; 1930 does not, with the second issue's page three scanned twice and its pages one and two never scanned. 160 column transcripts were reallocated. The cutting database still files them under the merged dates, and no corpus-wide sweep for further cases has been done.

The Frankenstein page. Before the orchestrator was split into place, reconcile and detect, a page could carry charts from one layout and boundaries from another. The fix was structural, not a threshold.

## 4. LLM transcription and classification (pre-1980)

### Shape

Three passes, each an agent definition in the repository with its prompt hashed onto every output row:

1. Column transcription. Each column image is sliced at the cutter's detected horizontal rules and sent to a Sonnet agent, which produces a diplomatic transcript – text as printed, with `[illegible]` and `[?word]` markers, curly quotes as printed, and no invented text – plus quality flags and, where the cut itself is wrong, a repair signal naming the fix.
2. Ad transcription. One agent per registered ad crop, prioritising advertiser, address, prices and goods.
3. Item classification. One text-only agent per page reads all its column and ad transcripts and returns the discrete items with types, summaries, character-offset spans and entity mentions. A fourth, structural pass merges over-segmented items and records where an article wraps around an inset ad, storing the polygon as a rectilinear union rather than a bounding box.

Repairs are a separate domain. Any pass can raise one; nothing ever mutates the cutting database automatically.

### Problems and fixes

Fabrication from downsampled images. The image-reading tool shrinks a tall column to about 250 pixels wide, and a model shown that will produce fluent, plausible, wrong text – names, prices, whole paragraphs. On the first test Haiku produced "RAGS for FACTS" and "PARSON'S for PAKENHAM". Slicing at horizontal rules before dispatch fixed it for both Sonnet and Haiku; the same Sonnet on the unsliced image "fabricated extensively despite the explicit instructions". The lesson as recorded: a no-fabrication rule cannot save a model that cannot see the text.

Slice-overlap duplication. The 20-pixel overlap between slices was transcribed twice and no joiner existed. Found in the 30 July quality review, fixed the same day with a deterministic resolver; 185 of 396 sliced rows were backfilled with the raw text preserved alongside.

Content-filter refusals. Serial fiction, a 1937 lumberjack essay and patent-medicine copy trip the output classifier. A four-tier ladder – whole column, per slice, Opus with a genre preamble, sub-slice subdivision – resolves most; the remainder go to a human import path. Only the first two tiers are automated.

The spontaneous zoom loop. 59% of agents wrote throwaway image-cropping scripts and re-read regions twenty to forty times chasing hard words, with a median of 39 tool calls against 7 for clean runs and no quality gain. An explicit prohibition plus an off-ramp ("the urge to zoom in is itself the stop signal") cut the outlier rate from 37% to 3.6%.

Silent gaps. Manual dispatch lost columns on two issues and nobody noticed until the user asked "have you stopped again?". Rule adopted: never derive outstanding work from memory; reconcile against the database every time.

Ticket inlining. Because a Workflow script cannot read files, an attempt to inline tickets made an agent regenerate them as output – six minutes and 100,000 to 150,000 tokens per issue. Reverted; claiming moved to plain Python at zero tokens.

## 5. The post-1980 problem

### Characterisation

The classical cutter was run on 1980 and the result was, in the user's words, beyond useless; the output was deleted. Rendering about 80 pages from ten issues across 1985 to 2007 established that this is a different paradigm, not a tuning problem, for the reasons given in section 1. Its aggregate statistics had reported 97% of pages with no quality flags. The note recorded at the time: the metrics share authorship with the code under review, so bad cuts the detector did not recognise as bad raise no flags. That warning has been quoted in three later documents.

### The modular cutter, May to June

An exploratory parallel pipeline was built over five days: the PDF's embedded text layer for geometry only, a masthead-band finder, headline versus pull-quote discrimination, article rectangles grown from headline runs, a column-grid consensus over adaptively placed bands, a negative-space pass that turns unclaimed ink into explicit orange regions rather than losing it, and a resolution stage for orphans and false ads. Eighty-one preview folders record its phases; a held-out run on 1986, 1998 and 2004 in June found the modular model held but borderless photos on low-resolution scans and classifieds pages were unsolved.

No phase notes were kept and no decision to stop was recorded. The entry point still says it does not write to production "once the visual output is approved", and approval never came. The August work started fresh on a different signal and reused two named functions from it as references. Its lasting legacy is the self-flattering-metrics lesson above.

### The OCR+LLM route, August

Tesseract had been dismissed early because of its layout weakness; the user noticed that cut columns bypass that weakness entirely. A single 1930 column matched a Sonnet transcript at 84% word level. A whole-page test then measured the production route at 1,636,302 tokens for seven columns against 147,782 for Tesseract plus an LLM correction pass – 9% of the cost.

For 1980 onward, where there are no column cuts, the route is: extract the native embedded page image, run Tesseract with Sauvola thresholding (Otsu lost the text under the fold shadow) and the best trained data, then two Sonnet passes per page. Cleanup corrects only low-confidence blocks, text only. Item segmentation groups the blocks into items by geometry first and looks at the page image only to confirm. The user's reaction to the first 2001 page: "exactly what we are looking for."

Two full issues ran clean:

| Issue | Pages | Items | Tokens | Minutes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1980-04-06 | 14 | 184 | 877,503 | 18.1 |
| 1990-10-10 | 16 | 390 | 1,053,837 | – |

Robustness fixes followed a three-agent audit: a page-level retry with explicit failure status and a damaged-page escalation after two failures; automatic recovery of OCR blocks that segmentation silently dropped (4 of 188 on one page) into an honestly flagged catch-all item at zero token cost; and stall watching, weaker here than on the column route because the agents make no incremental writes to observe.

## 6. Entities and terminology

Entity extraction was first done inline by the segmentation agent and then, on 9 August, split into its own batched, corpus-wide, decoupled pass. The user's framing was four units: page and OCR, cleanup and items, term extraction, reconciliation. The split dropped the candidate-list hinting that had softened spelling variation at extraction time; reconciliation became wholly the job of the later tiers, at the accepted cost of more near-duplicates to review.

The reconciliation tiers are: string heuristics (exact normalised match reliable; substring containment known noisy and never auto-applied), an automatic street-suffix merge verified against the live corpus before wiring in (one match, zero false positives), and a Haiku matching tier for variants no string rule can see, feeding a human review queue. Nothing auto-merges or auto-renames. Decisions are permanent and a deletion blocklist stops a rejected name being recreated.

Rules distilled from real misses: expand period abbreviations (Wm., Chas., Thos., Robt., Ed. with a guard) keeping the original as the mention text; never mint a bare-first-name person (29 purged); products genericise to the category with the brand in a manufacturer field; pick a name one level more specific than its type, neither repeating the type nor naming a one-off; Canadian spelling is canonical, after the user's correction "Don't be a cultural imperialist!".

Product types are grounded in Nomenclature for Museum Cataloging through its public SPARQL endpoint, storing the term, category, URI and bare catalogue number so the museum can correlate a newspaper mention with objects in its own collection. An early contains-match fallback produced confident wrong matches ("Coal Oil" to "charcoal", "Comfort Soap" to "comfort station") in an unattended run and was removed; matching is exact plus singular form only, precision over recall by design.

A readiness audit before a planned run found the reconciler would have sent a 1,900-name dictionary on its first call, and a checkpoint race that would silently and permanently skip any entity created between build and ingest. Both fixed and verified with a synthetic race.

## 7. The scaled experiment: structure from what Tesseract already knows

### Motivation

On 15 August the user set the direction: the Tesseract-first route was a large improvement, but the agent-based quality passes were still too expensive in time and tokens to scale, so build an experimental workflow using classical methods even more. The measurement that framed it:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Corpus pages | 70,063 |
| Pages done at the time | 562 (0.80%) |
| Tokens per page, OCR+LLM route | 77,233 (harness) to 103,710 (per-call sum) |
| Seconds per page | 93.4 |
| Share of tokens in item segmentation | 72% |
| Extrapolation | 5.4 to 7.3 billion tokens, about 76 days |

The two token figures disagree by about a third, probably cache-read accounting, and the record says not to build a cost model on either without naming which.

### The founding discovery

The hOCR parser read only word boxes and discarded everything else Tesseract emits: printed separator rules (a vertical one is a column boundary), photo regions, per-line font size, and Tesseract's own header, caption and float classes. All of it was already on disk. Recovered from 90 pages at zero OCR and zero LLM cost: 5,081 regions, 560 header lines, 447 caption lines. Going one layer deeper, Tesseract's layout analyser exposes block polygons (2,703 of 12,274 blocks are non-rectangular; an L-shaped story wrapping an ad has an L-shaped outline) and eleven block types against hOCR's three.

A related finding from reading Tesseract's source: its line classes are column-span verdicts, not typography. "Flowing text" means text that lives inside a column; "heading" means text spanning more than one. Verified on the corpus.

### The turn to typesetting

The first column attempt (band-first segmentation) cut escalation from 97.8% of pages to 31.5% and its best-scoring page held several articles in one 62%-high band. The user's correction reframed the whole stage: think like a typographer, the method is too complex for something so regular. The resulting reference document records how these pages were physically made – a pasteboard with the column grid pre-printed in non-reproducing blue, later desktop-publishing master pages; four numbers fixed per issue (margin, column width, gutter, count) in picas; every element an integer number of columns; ads sold by the column inch and dummied first, editorial filling the hole. The grid is an input to the page, not something to discover from it. A detector should fit a few parameters, not cluster freely and score each guess.

The column stage became a two-parameter fit (pitch and offset) with width derived, so the gutter is constant down the page by construction. Evidence is text blocks weighted by height, with rules and photos at half weight and rule edges crossed over into the neighbouring column. Median gutter came out at about half a percent of page width, one pica. A per-edge refinement pass made the gutter vary within the page on 61% of pages, following noise; archived, with the note that any retry must stay parametric.

### Boxes from corners alone

Boxed zones – ads, notices, panels – went through three generations in a day. Naive corner matching found only 22% of rule endpoints within 1% of each other, because printed boxes have rounded corners and the sides genuinely never meet. A four-sided detector allowing for rounded corners, drop shadows (28 pixels top, 48 bottom on one box) and stacked boxes sharing verticals worked but accumulated six tuned thresholds. The user's instruction, from an experiment colouring grid cells where separator ends coincide: work from the corners. The final derivation has one predicate – a rectangle is an item when no other corner interrupts its sides – which rejects bridges, gutter slivers and unions of any depth by construction. It works in grid cells, which are square, because page percent is two units (x of width, y of height) and mixing them lost a real box. Content is recorded on every zone but never decides: geometry decides, content is evidence, since 28.8% of real boxes hold no text block.

A limit found later: the corner map is built from rule ends, so a grid ruled with continuous lines (a 1986 page with 12 of 18 intersections mid-span) is nearly invisible to it.

### Other stages

Rim sliver removal (binding gutter and sheet edge arrive as separators and photos): 537 removed over 90 pages, false-positive proxy 5.6% against 31.6% for the band test it replaced. Two rules make it work: a sliver may not be corroborated by another sliver, and the edge tested is the one the sliver runs parallel to.

Content area: left and right by agreement among survivors, top and bottom by extreme, outer perimeter with every margin floored at four cells. Items outside the box fell from 14.5% to 5.7%. Finding it exposed a double write – a later stage had been overwriting the top and bottom on 84 of 90 pages – and the record says the lesson is the discrepancy, not the bug.

Photos that are actually ads: line count separates them cleanly; 86 conversions on 42 pages. Photo-and-caption pairing by geometry: 45% of photos captioned. Horizontal alignments with column spans: built, measured as a real but modest 1.65× lift, and withdrawn on the user's assessment that it was not helping anything.

A vision-only side test (the model looks at the page, no code) cost 38,000 to 87,000 tokens for one page – the same order as the thing being replaced – and was closed. Worth keeping: only Opus derived the 8-column grid; Sonnet cost 28% more than Opus and scored worse. Tesseract tuning for missed rules was ruled out by a controlled test: five configurations, identical separator output every time while the OCR text changed.

### The IIIF viewer

Every stage publishes as annotations on IIIF manifests, viewed in an embedded Mirador. Four other viewers were tried or assessed: TIFY does not render these overlays (a predicted fix was falsified on device), Theseus is hosted-only, Clover and Universal Viewer do not support annotations. Mirador's default filter excludes the annotation motivation the project uses, which is why it is embedded and configured rather than linked. The site sits behind Cloudflare Access, so hosted third-party viewers cannot reach the manifests anyway.

### Process failures, recorded

The experiment's design record lists seven, and says they are one failure in different coats – substituting a number or a local check for looking at the artefact:

1. Shipping on a metric without rendering. Relaxing the box rule from four corners to three improved every statistic (547 to 812 boxes, under-finding pages 31 to 18) and the render was a thicket of near-duplicate rectangles. Reverted on sight.
2. Changing the test to fit the fix, and losing the largest box on the page for two further changes.
3. "Fixing" what the printer did – splitting a continuous column rule into per-box segments, inventing gaps never in the ink.
4. Accretive patching instead of questioning the approach.
5. Re-litigating a settled direction after being told content confirms and geometry decides.
6. Not re-checking the whole page after each change.
7. Treating page percent as one unit.

The remedy stated: render the whole page, before reporting anything, every time.

### Prior art

Nothing off the shelf emits background geometry (whitespace, gutters) rather than foreground regions. If a whitespace detector is ever built the record points at an expired Google patent's tall-narrow-window filter rather than projection profiles. And there is no evaluation standard for whitespace detection and no layout ground truth for newspapers after 1950 anywhere; the closest public set is 528 pre-1950 European pages.

## 8. Newsgrid

Between 16 and 19 August a separate line was developed in a Claude chat session, outside this repository, and handed over on 31 August as a self-contained bundle. It is a pure OpenCV script: deskew, binarise, find strokes, build bordered items from rule pairs verified by perimeter coverage, find imagery from an illumination-modelled paper map, vote gutters over horizontal bands and fit a pitch and phase, then group display type into headline blocks. No OCR. Its output is a JSON layout skeleton and an annotated overlay, intended to guide a downstream transcription stage orchestrated in n8n.

It differs from the scaled pipeline in technique (pixels, not Tesseract's regions) but shares its principles: visible boundaries only – ink the design put on the page separates things, and the notional grid never gates grouping, only reports extent; bordered items are islands whose interiors do not vote; every pixel constant scales with working width. Three regimes are gated by measurement rather than date: the tuned regime for phone photos and 1997 scans, an archive regime for 200 DPI masters, and a bitonal regime detected from the grey histogram. A ruled-era variant was validated on one 1902 page and parked, on the view that different eras need a different take.

The handover's discipline is a regression protocol: five reference pages must reproduce byte-identical JSON, and eleven archive and bitonal pages must hold their supported gutters within two pixels and their box and image counts. Run on the project machine on 31 August it did not pass: four of five reference pages identical, one differing in a single vote value; four of seven archive pages passing, with the two the briefing already called fragile shifting pitch. Library-version drift was suspected, not established, and no rebaselining was done. The bundle is untracked in git.

Its backlog, in the briefing's own priority order: zonal grid fitting for pages that mix an editorial column system with an ad-card grid; residual reporting that separates misfit from stray votes; high-key bitonal photographs; sparse line art; an accidental masthead-chaining guard that should become a principled screened-background test.

## 10. Insights that emerged

The grid is an input. Newspaper pages were made on a fixed physical grid measured in picas; deviations are more likely scan noise, a photo or an ad interior than a new column width. Fit a few numbers, do not discover boundaries. Vertical position, by contrast, is not quantised, because ads were sold by the inch.

Geometry decides, content is evidence. Deciding on content – a box with no text is not an ad – throws away a third of real boxes. Record content, decide on ink.

Visible boundaries only. Rules, borders, bars and whitespace gaps the designer put on the page separate things. Notional boundaries such as a fitted grid never should. Stated by the user when a headline-grouping fix had oscillated for days; it resolved the oscillation.

Do not fix what the printer did. A column rule spanning several stacked ads is one continuous strip they butt against. Rounded corners, drop shadows and double rules are production facts, and correcting them removes real structure.

Render before believing. Every serious regression in the layout work improved a statistic. Scores computed by the pipeline under review cannot be trusted to judge it; the page can.

A flag that cannot fire is worse than no flag. A nesting flag that read zero against a dozen real nestings was quoted as evidence of a clean derivation.

Page percent is two units. A threshold written once and applied to both axes is silently anisotropic by the page's aspect ratio. Work in square cells.

Tesseract's layout output is worth more than its text. The rules, photo regions, block polygons and block types it already computes are free structure that the standard hOCR path discards.

A model cannot transcribe what it cannot see. Downsampling produces confident fabrication that no prompt rule prevents. Slice first.

Diplomatic transcription with honest silences beats plausible completeness, and confidence should be a count of markers, not a self-rating.

Decouple in time. Entity extraction, classification and reconciliation as batched corpus-wide passes, independent of which issue or when, proved simpler and more robust than inline extraction.

Cost is not monotonic in model size. On the vision test Sonnet cost more than Opus and scored worse; on transcription Haiku was fast and hallucinated fifteen times per column. Sonnet for images, Haiku for text-only judgement, Opus for hard cases.

Economise on the transcripts, not on the tools. The user's rule after shortcuts were taken in dev tooling: tokens are for production runs; render freely and check everything while building.

Preserve the journey. No prototype, render or preview page is deleted; new versions are published alongside. The 84 preview folders and the dated newsgrid lineage are the evidence of the R&D path.

## 11. Current state and open questions

Done and stable: the pre-1980 cutting pipeline and its full-corpus run; the column transcription route with its retry ladder and factory orchestration; the OCR+LLM route for 1980 onward with retry, damaged-page tracking and orphan recovery; the entity registry, review pages and terminology cleanup; the scaled pipeline stages 1a to 2c with IIIF output for seven issues.

Not done: item classification on 59 transcribed pre-1980 issues (ad crops in cold storage); 647 route-B items awaiting term extraction; the scaled experiment's agreed next four steps (columns refit with boxes removed, article-versus-ad zones, horizontals re-tracked, joining non-boxed content into items – the 72% prize) not started; the block splitter for the 25% of "flowing text" blocks that cross a column; the newsgrid regression not passing on this machine and its bundle untracked.

Open decisions the record does not settle:

- The relationship between the two layout-analysis tracks. The scaled pipeline and newsgrid share a goal and principles and differ in technique. No measured comparison exists and no decision on merge, parallel or compare is recorded.
- Ground truth. There is none for post-1950 newspaper layout anywhere. The last committed recommendation was to hand-mark two or three pages so that precision and recall can be reported honestly.
- The effort-level lever on transcription, six times faster at low effort, has not been checked for accuracy.
- A corpus-wide sweep for further merged issues.
